Case study 01 — Manufacturing

David Jackson & Son.

Website rebuildProduction schedulerStrategy deck

The situation

A Shropshire joinery firm where production was planned on paper and in people's heads, and the website no longer reflected what the business had become. Both needed doing — by someone who would still be there afterwards.

What I built

  • A rebuilt website, designed and written from scratch.
  • An eight-stage drag-and-drop production scheduler with automatic breach detection, used to plan real jobs on the factory floor.
  • A written strategy deck taking the findings to the people who make the decisions.

How it was run

Scoped in writing before a line of code. Delivered in milestones against fixed dates. Handed over with the client owning the domain, the code and the content outright.

Outcome — to be added. One measurable result and one approved client quote will do more work than everything else on this page.

Engineering detail

  • Eight production stages, drag-and-drop rescheduling, automatic breach detection against stage deadlines.
  • Migrated from localStorage to a Supabase backend — the plan survives the browser and is shared by everyone.
  • Sign-in protected by two-factor authentication, so the plan and the customer names behind it stay private.
  • Autosave on every change, with one-click backup and restore — nobody loses an afternoon's planning to a closed tab.
  • GitHub → Vercel pipeline: every change reviewable, every change reversible.

Case study 02 — Automotive

WulfTek Tuning.

Website rebuildDVLA APIConversion designMarket positioning

The situation

Mobile ECU remapping across Telford and Shropshire, with a website that could have belonged to any tuning firm in the country. Black background, red accents, "UNLOCK YOUR TRUE POWER", three headline percentages, and three competing buttons in the hero. Nothing on it was specific to this business, this county, or this customer — and nothing on it asked the question the customer was actually turning up with.

What I changed

  • Rebuilt the site around one question — "What could your car actually do?" — and one input: your registration.
  • Wired the hero to the DVLA vehicle API, so entering a plate returns that specific vehicle rather than a generic sales pitch.
  • Replaced the stock dark-tuning look with real workshop photography and an editorial type hierarchy — the business's own premises doing the reassurance work.
  • Opened a service line nobody local was serving: agricultural remapping.
Outcome — to be added. Enquiry volume before and after, plus the client's own words, once WulfTek have approved them.

The reg box, and why it's that big

  • It costs nothing to start. No name, no email, no phone — the smallest possible first action, taken before any commitment is asked for.
  • It's already familiar. Styled as an actual UK plate, so it needs no instruction. Every driver knows what goes in a yellow box with a blue UK flag.
  • It's about them, not the firm. "What could your car do" is a question about the visitor's own vehicle, not a claim about the business.
  • Value arrives before the ask. Real numbers for their car come first; booking is offered afterwards, once something has already been given.
  • One action, not three. The old hero offered Book, WhatsApp and Get a quote side by side — three doors is a decision, and a decision is friction.

Answering the real objection

  • The thing that stops people booking a remap isn't price — it's fear of what it does to the engine.
  • So directly beneath the call to action: original file always kept, nothing removed from your engine, reversible at any time.
  • Three sentences, placed at the exact moment of hesitation, addressing the objection the old site never acknowledged.

Why the old site read like every other one

A generator can write a website in a minute.

What it can't do is spend an afternoon in the unit finding out that half the machinery within twenty miles is agricultural and nobody local is remapping it.

The old WulfTek site had every hallmark of copy written without anyone being asked anything: interchangeable superlatives, round percentages with no test data behind them, a location line naming three places and saying nothing about any of them. It could have been lifted onto a competitor's domain without changing a word — which is the actual problem, whatever produced it.

I use modern tooling constantly, and I'd be a hypocrite to pretend otherwise. The difference isn't the tools. It's that the words came after the visit.

Found on site, not in a brief

  • Agricultural remapping. Tractors, telehandlers and farm machinery across Shropshire — plenty of demand, essentially nobody local marketing to it. It surfaced in conversation during the first visit and became a service line.
  • The reversibility fear. Not on any brief. It came from what customers actually ask before booking, and it's now answered in the hero.
  • The plate as the opening move. Everyone in the trade already asks for a registration. The site just asks for it first, and gives something back for it.
